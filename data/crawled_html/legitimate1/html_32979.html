Two Decades of Calendar

December 13, 2025 @ 10:25 pm · Filed under Calendar for WordPress, Development

Calendar for WordPress quietly turned 20 years old around a month ago – yes, you read that correctly, two whole decades of event management across thousands of websites worldwide. There are dozens of calendar plugins these days and the WordPress ecosystem is orders of magnitude bigger than it was back in 2006, but it is a source of great pride to have not only been in on the ground floor, but to still be actively supporting those early users who helped me back then by testing and deploying what was the first calendaring plugin for WordPress.

Over the years I have been contacted by hundreds of individuals from around the world. Some have reported bugs or security issues, others have enquired if I can add an event to their church website (I can’t) and many more have simply thanked me for a free resource that has helped them. I want to take this opportunity to thank you all for keeping my faith in open source software alive.

A birthday and a special one at that shouldn’t be without a present, so today I release Calendar 1.3.17. Granted, the release is nothing ground breaking, simply keeping the show on the road, fixing an issue or two and tidying up a few loose ends, but it’s a testament to the power and enduring nature of open source, not to mention my personal commitment to the movement.

Retreat Reflections

November 15, 2025 @ 9:02 pm · Filed under Meditation, My life

The last of the summer sun sank down beneath the horizon, casting the edges of the billowing white clouds, which were still vibrant against the azure blue sky, a rich colour of crimson. The effect of viewing the sunset through a distant tree line had the mesmerising illusion of lighting thousands of dancing candles in the branches as they gently swayed in the breeze. I stood there transfixed. Time seemed to dilate and it’s difficult to say how long I was unaware of anything else around me, but suffice it to say that the daylight was notably reduced when I turned round and headed back to the monastery dormitory, reflecting that this would be the last time I would see the sun set at this time of night for nearly 6 months – the clocks due to be rolled back to Greenwich Mean Time overnight.

Just 24 hours into a 5 day silent retreat under the direction of an experienced Ajahn (a monastic teacher with a minimum of 10 years in robes), I realised that I was doing precisely what one is instructed to attempt during such sojourns – to exist completely in the moment and direct the mind gently to only the here and now. I really wouldn’t have chosen to be anywhere else and it felt deeply peaceful, akin to sand settling on the bed of a lake and the waters of the mind becoming clearer as a result.

It would be deeply misleading if I were to intimate that retreat life is all like this. The mind has had years of conditioning to depend on external stimuli and it is only a matter of time before the rebel gets up on his milk crate and sows the seeds of revolution and anarchy. For me this began that very night after evening chanting. “What if you oversleep the morning gong due to the clock change?”… “You might not be able to sleep at all”… “What did the Ajahn just say? Stop thinking about having another cup of tea!”…”You’ve sat for hundreds of hours of meditation on this cushion, why is you leg suddenly hurting now?”. I returned from my reverie to the present at just the opportune moment to hear a wise and timely reminder from the Ajahn – “…it [the retreat] is your opportunity to investigate the here and now, to witness your mind and body in the world at this moment and to realise the nature of the way things are, that it’s like this”

The tone was thus set for the remainder of the retreat; a resolution to simply observe the way things were without judgement or mindlessly trying to change them. I might not be successful, but I was going to try. If I failed, I would witness my failure, how it felt and try again.

During subsequent periods of almost entirely unguided, seated meditation, the pain in my legs returned. I watched as the stiffness oscillated through my calves like waves on an ocean. The strange thing about observing your own pain purely as a witness is that by removing the “I” in the equation, you don’t experience the sensations in a monotonous fashion. The way the anatomy feels is not fixed, occasionally an intense discomfort simply disappears, at other times it is replaced by different sensations elsewhere. It wasn’t comfortable, but it would have been wrong to describe it as discomfort either, after all I wasn’t in control of how my legs felt, merely the consciousness required to watch the sensations. Without fail though, the bell would be rung after such a session and once a respectful stillness had been kept for the departure of the Ajahn, the solidity in my legs would make the first few steps difficult, stiff and in staccato. I watched that too.

At times we were invited to participate in walking meditation in the monastery woodland. Just prior to one such session, the Ajahn recounted a story where the Buddha is walking in the forest with a number of his monks. Picking up a handful of dried fallen Autumn leaves he asks them how they would feel if he were to burn them to ashes. The monks shrugged and expressed indifference to the idea. The Buddha responds by stating “this is how you should feel about your own body”.

As I watched the sensations in my feet and legs as one foot followed the other in the crunching of leaf litter under the still radiant Autumn foliage of the forest, I couldn’t help but reflect on the words of the story before the session. Are we then to be depressed when walking in the beauty of the forest by causing our minds to dwell on our own mortality? Then it dawned on me. The beauty of the forest is *in spite* of the fact that the leaves are destined to be crushed underfoot, that the trees will eventually fall. It is in the nature of woodland to grow, change and die – yet it is nonetheless a beautiful experience to walk amongst it during its life. This is why we must live our lives in the each moment; we do not know how many our days are numbered and yet irrespective of whether they be many or few, *this* day exists and we can take it just as it is regardless of where we came from or where we are going. The wind gently rustled the branches overhead as golden leaves floated down around my head, brushing my face like tears as they fell to the ground. I finished my circuit of the forest and as I approached the monastery grounds once more, smiled contentedly inwards as the realisation dawned that I had thought about nothing but the colour and sound of the leaves for the past 20 minutes.

Meal times within a monastic setting are important. Partaking in food after midday is not allowed for the monks and as guests of the monastery, retreat attendees also keep this particular precept. The effect on the mind and body is fascinating to observe. As the monks chant thanks for the alms prior to the lay people collecting their food, I can hear the gurgles and grumbles of my own stomach as I wait for my turn to join the queue. Selecting portions is suddenly transformed into a big task; yes, you’re hungry and won’t eat dinner, so you need enough food, but you can only eat so much in one sitting, so how much to put on your plate? Food is generously given by the community either directly or in the form of monetary donation, so the last thing a guest would wish to do is waste anything. The mind swirls intensely around this most basic of dilemmas. I watch it do so and then smile to myself, a narrative forming in my mind “This is what it is to have enough delicious food to eat and yet still agonise over if you’ve got the selection right – it’s like this”. As I set my spoon down in the empty bowl and allow the food to settle in my stomach before joining the washing up line, realisation dawns that I will not be availing of the usual postprandial cup of tea – my portion size calculations were a little too generous and the sensation in my stomach says as much. I watch the sensations of digestion as I leave the kitchen area, after all – it’s like this.

As an introvert silence is not a problem for me. I’ve done longer retreats before and yet, sometimes, even my own mind throws me some surprises. Taking a shower one afternoon an inexplicable but palpable anxiety washes over me along with the soap suds. I immediately begin to internally question it in a brusque manner – “why on Earth would you feel anxious on a silent mediation retreat, where you’re well fed, have a comfortable warm place to sleep, like minded people and teachers who care for your wellbeing? what a foolish thing to feel… you need to get rid of that right away”. Then I remember the Ajahn’s words near the start of the retreat. The soliloquy changes – “This is what it is to feel inexplicably anxious on a meditation retreat – it’s like this”. The warm water washes over me and almost immediately the nature of the sensations change. The feeling is still there, the label is still the same, the inexplicable nature is still inexplicable, but I’m not the anxious feeling – I am witness to the anxious feeling and as a witness I can observe it arise, linger and pass away. It does so during my self practice yoga session at the end of the dormitory and I’m reminded of the many teachers who explain that cessation lies in the nature of attachment. The moment I let go of the need to be rid of an anxious sensation, it was able to run its course and drift away into the ether.

Ending a retreat is always an interesting experience. You come to know your fellow participants purely by their actions – the “noble silence” dictating that, for the time being, you will not know them by speech. After the meal is served a slow ripple of sound cascades across the tables and chairs. Where was once just the clinking of cutlery and scraping of chairs, now there is the excitement of conversation – “how was it for you?”… “did you go into the woods?”… “weren’t the sunsets beautiful?”… “what did you feel about the way the Ajahn taught…?”…”do you keep up a continuity of practice at home?”. The vocal chords feel strained, tight but the mind wills them on. Phone numbers are exchanged on pieces of paper as our phones are still switched off, some resolving to keep them that way for a few more hours and allow “re-entry” into the non monastic world more slowly.

Re-united with my wife we exchange a few words, focusing more on the smiles we give each other and the gift of looking into each others eyes again – the light of even a few grains of wisdom glinting there for each other to see. It is customary for the Ajahn to receive thanks from retreat participants in the Sangha room after lunch (Sangha is a word meaning assembly and it refers to the community of monks both in a single monastery and also around the world), so we squeeze in at the back and await our opportunity to speak and give thanks. Speech still feels alien and my usually articulate brain struggles to say things in my usually polished way. I set this aside mentally and deliver my thanks as best I can in the moment, reflecting that “this is what it is to be truly grateful to a learned teacher but still struggle for words – it’s like this”. My words were received with thanks all the same and we enjoy a pleasant 20 minutes or so seated with others, listening to further exchanges of words of wisdom and the retreat reflections of other participants.

Taking the wheel to drive home I recall that feeling near the start of our stay where the silt was settling and the mind consequently gained clarity. I feel this again in the context of the teachings. In a monastic setting one usually spends more time reflecting on what is said, but now, out in the world, it is time to put this into practice with renewed vigour – to be a witness to life rather than allowing it to pass us by. But if something does pass us by, to just note and reflect – after all, it’s like this.

Tube Strike

September 13, 2025 @ 11:31 am · Filed under Musings

This week London has been enduring yet another tube strike. Every year there seems to be a new dispute, a new reason to make the lives of travelling Londoners a misery. On this occasion the autumn rains had well and truly set in and bedraggled commuters negotiated cycle lanes, thronging pavements and long bus queues trying to get about their business – all the while the warm and dry of the subterranean rail network was silent.

I think what grates on me the most about transport strikes is how at odds the workers in these fields are with other members of the working class. While warehouse operatives and cleaners continue to endure a punishing cost of living crisis while earning barely more than the minimum wage, train drivers make these very people late for their precarious jobs while demanding yet another inflation busting pay rise on top of those they have received in the past.

The current fixed salary for a tube driver is £68,000 – a near whopping 90% excess on the UK average wage of £37,000. To force folk earning much less to endure hardship is simply unjustifiable. TFL workers are complaining that they can’t afford a mortgage on a house in London for their wages, while coolly ignoring the millions of other Londoners who’s livelihoods their strikes are threatening and who would never be able to afford to buy a house either but who, interestingly, are not out on strike. The word we’re looking for is selfish.

Personally I think the time is nigh to make the most of some of the stellar progress we have made in their field of automation and to thus release London from the stranglehold of the tube strike gravy train for good. The DLR opened in 1987 and uses a network of automated trains. With safety considerations never having been more prominent in rail signal control, we can build systems that it is almost impossible for a human driver to breach – think automated breaking on passing a red light etc.

With the TFL bill for just the Piccadilly line upgrade projected to exceed 3 billion pounds by the time the project concludes, I feel their priorities need some adjustment. A single strike can cost the London economy 230 million pounds and the projected cost of fully automating the Piccadilly line is 4.9 billion. My feeling is that we should be postponing the new trains in favour of investing in automation. The future savings in terms of staff and economic impact to London will more than make up for the difference over time – TFL reported a staffing spend of nearly 3 billion pounds last year.

In international politics a nation state will always try to avoid being held over a barrel by another nation in terms of military or economic might. It is high time for TFL to do the same in respect of the ransom the good citizens of London are regularly held to by those who already earn plenty for the work they do.

Autumn Rain

August 30, 2025 @ 8:51 pm · Filed under My life

After what has arguably been a truly excellent summer by British standards, the hot balmy weather finally broke in the early hours of Friday morning. There is something truly magical about being tucked up warm and cosy somewhere, listening to the sound of the rain; the soft patter the first drops make on the leaves of plants patiently waiting for a drink, sheets of rain from the gathering storm cascading into rapidly forming puddles on the roadways and torrents flooding through the down spouts and into the almost empty water barrels on the terrace with a thundering roar.

Conventional wisdom indicates that if black clouds still exist overhead and the showers show no sign of abating then the walk up to the office is to be dreaded, but with trusty umbrella in hand and the scent of petrichor on the heavy London pre-autumnal air, I set off with a spring in my step.

The drumming of the rain on the black fabric of the umbrella set the beat to my walking pace as I reminisced of nights out under canvas as a boy while on some scouting expedition, safe and dry in a tent with the smell of wood smoke from the previous evening’s camp fire still in my nostrils.

It is true that I arrived at my office somewhat wetter than when I had started out, indeed my shoes showed no signs of being amenable to completely drying out in the designated basement changing area, but I started work happy. The changing seasons are a wonderful way of marking our precious time on this earth; neither too fast to notice nor too far off to ignore, the changing scenery and accompanying physical sensations of each new season can never fail to bring joy to the heart.

Hiking in Snowdonia

June 23, 2025 @ 11:27 pm · Filed under Hiking

Shweta and I took advantage of the sunny weather and spent a long weekend up in Snowdonia. Our friend Steffan kindly put us up in his home and we spent 3 days trying to get as much scrambling in as possible. The weather conditions turned out to be as variable as you might expect in the mountains; squally showers on the way up Crib Goch, glorious sunshine as we pushed onto the summit of Snowdon and then mysterious rolling mists as we were beaten back off the final ridge of the Snowdon Horseshoe. As well as a very windy ascent of the South side of Tryfan, we spent a day on the South coast of the Llyn Peninsula and took in one of our favourite walks there. Last but most certainly not least we were able to enjoy a proper evening in with Steffan and his partner Dennis – sharing some home cooked food and sharing life stories into the evening as the rain lashed down at the end of our second day. All in all a splendid flying visit to one of our favourite places in the world.

Comments Open Forever On WordPress Image Uploads

June 15, 2025 @ 2:49 pm · Filed under Computers & WWW, WordPress

If you’re using WordPress as an image gallery you’ll have noticed that you get the rather nice option of being able to leave comments on individual photos within a gallery. This is certainly a useful feature, but I recently discovered that it comes with a rather odd bug.

In the WordPress admin panel, under the discussion section, you can choose to close comments on old articles and select a number of days after which to do so. Given the tendency for spam to reign supreme, enabling this feature is a smart move and significantly reduces the spam attack surface, while still allowing a good chunk of time for discussion on new articles.

Enter the bug; despite the requisite time having elapsed, image attachment comments remain open forever! This was actually reported in a bug on WordPress core trac back in 2012, but after being open and discussed for the best part of 2 years it was closed on account of there being an option to control comments on each attachment. Now don’t get me wrong, granular control is always welcome, but if you’re running a photo gallery on your site with thousands of snaps, you’d rather not have to manually open each one after 30 days (or whatever your desired comment window is) and close them!

Never fear though, the fix is here. Simply add the following filter invocation to your theme functions.php file and comments will close on image attachments after the same delay as they do on ordinary posts. Happy snapping!


function kjo_close_all_comments_after_delay($post_types) {

    if ($post_types != null) {

        array_push($post_types, 'attachment');

    }

    return $post_types;

}

add_filter('close_comments_for_post_types', 'kjo_close_all_comments_after_delay');

Demise of the Supermarket

June 8, 2025 @ 7:10 pm · Filed under Musings

Growing up I lived around the corner from our local supermarket. This meant it was commonplace to accompany my Mother on the weekly food shop. While I don’t recall being particularly enthused about traipsing around the store, I do remember a lot about the shopping experience; the smell of freshly baked bread wafting towards you when the main doors slid open, the friendly faces behind the tills, the counter staff at the back of the store serving out wedges, slices, cuts and fillets of cheese, bread, meat and fish respectively.

I don’t want to go so far as to say there was a community feel, although regular shoppers and staff certainly did get to know each other over the years, but there was a real sense that the store wanted you, the customer, to be there – really valuing your presence and seeking to make the shopping experience as positive as it could be.

It’s tempting to look back and think we lived in a constant world, but of course we didn’t. Even back then radical changes happened we all had to adapt to. At some point my Mother went from paying by cheque to using a debit card. Remarkably I actually still remember the weekend that our local supermarket changed from price tickets on products to barcodes – on the Friday cashiers were keying in the prices of products, come Monday morning there were barcodes and beeps. The one thing that didn’t change though was the customer experience – beeps didn’t matter if the same friendly face was behind the till.

Fast forward into adulthood and I too became a patron of a supermarket local to where I lived. There were definitely changes since my childhood experiences. The staffed desk to which you took your fruit and vegetables to be weighed was gone, replaced by scales integrated into the tills. The overriding essence of the experience however remained remarkably unchanged; the smell of baking bread, staffed tills and counter service at the back of the store, these days enhanced by the addition of a pizza counter and salad bar. That feeling you were welcome as a customer ever present.

I’m unclear exactly when the feeling in my local supermarket began to change. The trouble with places you frequent is that subtle but iterative changes can so easily pass you by, but at some point the realisation dawned and once your eyes are open, a trickle suddenly feels like a flood.

My local supermarket played host to a particularly special fishmonger; he represented the supermarket chain with the Marine Stewardship Council (MSC) and attended their conferences. He brought his experience and knowledge with him back into the store, regularly discussing the merits of various fish and answering customer queries as to environmental concerns. One day I attended the store to find the fish counter closed and the experienced fishmonger relegated to adding toppings to dough bases behind the pizza counter. To say I was shocked was an understatement, but the scary thing is no one seemed to notice. Customers just picked up packaged fish from the adjacent refrigerated shelves and carried on with their shopping – it felt uncomfortable, uneasy.

Visible changes seemed to come thick and fast after that. Other service counters started to close or become staffed only on a part time basis. Staffed tills began to be replaced with so called automated tills. Initially we had the option to choose a staffed till at all times. Then staffed tills too became part time; if you were in the store of an evening you had to check out yourself, even if you had a full trolley. Throughout all of this what became apparent was the caring previously demonstrated to customers by staff had also evaporated. Can a customer really feel valued if, fully trolley in hand, they are given short shrift when requesting staff open a till.

I wish the tale of woe ended here, but sadly it doesn’t. Indifference towards customers slowly morphed into hostile suspicion. Trolleys had coin slots added to them. A 4 foot steel fence was erected at the front of the store that upon completing the self checkout process you could only egress through by scanning your printed receipt. If the self checkout machine didn’t print you one, you were cast dirty looks by the attendant staff when you asked to be released from the pen without presenting the requisite piece of paper.

The fence, that was the last straw. I work hard, I pay my taxes. I deserve not to be treated like a criminal just buying my weekly groceries. But where could I go? Seemingly all supermarkets had veered off down this questionable path full of thorns and briars to ensnare the unwary, but there was one option – online. Seeking to distance myself from the physical supermarkets that had misunderstood the requirement for customer relations so dramatically, I selected an online only grocer, placed an order and waited to see what the experience would be like.

It’s true, my nostalgia for a supermarket that looks after its customers and is a moderately enticing place to be is certainly not satisfied with the point and click replacement, but the service you do get? Like night and day.

Delivery slots can be reserved in advance and a purchased pack of delivery slots work out at only one pound per delivery, significantly cheaper than the weekly fuel cost for driving to the physical store, not to mention the dreaded ULEZ stealth tax. Then there is the time saving; I quite literally have my Monday evenings back again – battling through the traffic, selecting all the products, scanning everything, packing it all, getting back home again – all coolly replaced with a soft knock at the door and bags of groceries already separated by freezer, fridge and cupboard category – amazing. Delivery staff are friendly, you get a text to tell you when they are on their way and what van they will be in. If all of that wasn’t enough, if a product isn’t right or there is any issue, the customer service representatives operate a presumption of innocence – one doesn’t have to prove you have an issue, a refund is given without question – a far cry from the fence that stubbornly blocked your path until you proved your lack of criminality.

Do I miss the real supermarket? Honestly? Not at all. They had their chance to keep things operating properly and they spectacularly blew it. Their online competitors fill the gap nicely and we customers have got an option to feel valued again.

This article may well beg the question for you – is this just the way of the world now? Well, I’m sorry to say that no, it isn’t. Our continental cousins still have real supermarkets with the scent of fresh bread, counter service and staffed tills. A recent trip through France necessitated a stop at a supermarket and I honestly felt like a child again. It’s proof that where there is a will there is a way and so sadly one must conclude that, in the UK at least, the will is conspicuous by its absence.

Neff Dishwasher E22 Error

June 4, 2025 @ 8:08 pm · Filed under DIY, Electrical

Cursory searches online for the E22 error raised by a Neff dishwasher imply that something has stuck in the pump preventing the machine from draining. While this has once been the case when encountering this error, what it actually means in the strictest sense of the sensor code is that a larger than expected level of water remains in the sump at the termination of the selected cycle.

This slightly more nuanced description of the error means two very important things; 1) you likely do not need to re-run your wash cycle and dishes are liable to be perfectly clean at after a cycle ending in an E22 fault, 2) it is not usually necessary to go ferreting around the pump sump with your fingers searching for a blockage – merely draining the water from the sump will be enough. I’ll provide photos and instructions for how to drain the sump with a minimum of fuss.

Firstly you need to remove the lower basket so you can clearly access the filter panel that protrudes from the base of the machine, under the sprayer arm.

Twist the protruding filter counter-clockwise around a quarter of a turn and the panel and filter will lift up. If this is dirty you can clean it off and rinse it, but if not, just set it to one side.

Finally take a syringe and begin to suck the water sitting in the sump out. I purchased a large syringe specifically for this purpose; it permits the removal of water right down to the bottom of the sump without getting your hands dirty. It is important that you remove as much water as possible so that the error doesn’t return on the next cycle.

And that’s all folks! Now the morning drama of being presented with an E22 can be resolved in a mere 5 minutes with your dishwasher ready to take the breakfast crockery in a jiffy.

Park Life

May 31, 2025 @ 8:08 pm · Filed under Images, My life

Today Shweta and I headed out on our bicycles to make the most of the summer weather which has finally graced us with its presence. With the thermometer showing a balmy 25 degrees we had the confidence for the first time this year to leave the jumpers at home!

Seeking to stick as close as possible to the Thames path South of the river to the East of Tower Bridge, we were treated to a very pleasant cycle route. Officially marked paths occasionally give way to tree lined quiet lanes which almost feel like secret gardens hidden in the heart of the city. With the sun dappling our faces as we pedalled beneath the branches it couldn’t have felt more perfect.

We’d packed a picnic and headed for Greenwich Park. A large chestnut tree just down the hill from the observatory offered us shade from the heat of the day while we watched the people of London enjoy their own little slice of summer; ball games, dogs chasing sticks, couples like us whiling away the afternoon.

We eventually departed for home desirous of a cup of tea, but the ride back home was similarly pleasant – the scents of family barbecues wafting towards us from behind the fence panels of Rotherhithe houses. There are moments in all seasons that ensure I cherish the changing weather patterns, but I will certainly remember today as one of the finer ways to spend a lazy summer afternoon.
