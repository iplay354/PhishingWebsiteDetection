Helping communities to help rivers

The world's rivers have never been under so much stress.

Even in the UK and Ireland, rivers are under huge pressure from water extraction ("abstraction"), climate change, pollution, and other human activities.

The UK Rivers Network brings people together to create a better future for rivers and inland waters in the UK and Ireland.

December 2025

18 December 2025: Teamwork secures milestone in Hampshire River Test restoration: Miles of chalk stream habitat in and around the Test in Hampshire have been improved in one of the UK's most substantial river restoration schemes, overseen by Wessex Rivers Trust.

18 December 2025: Marine Conservation Society: year in review: This year has been one of milestones for the Marine Conservation Society. Here's their round-up of recent Westminster wins, including the Water (Special Measures) Act.

17 December 2025: Don Catchment: Best moments of 2025: The Don Catchment Rivers Trust (DCRT) offers a choice selection of memorable moments from the past year.

17 December 2025: Why sewage can be a distraction: River Action launches new strategy: Despite popular claims that sewage is the leading cause of the UK river’s poor health, agriculture is the biggest culprit, affecting 45% of water bodies. This is the starting point for River Action's new Water Pollution Strategy, which focuses attention more firmly on agricultural impacts.

17 December 2025: The Riverfly Partnership: a year of collaboration, conferences, and citizen science: 2025 has been another fantastic and busy year for the Riverfly Partnership: "We’ve seen the volunteer network grow, reaching new geographical locations with new groups coming aboard from the rivers of Cornwall to the west coast of Wales, over to the Northern Ireland and all the way up to the Highlands of Scotland. Our geographical reach is larger than ever with over 700 rivers now being monitored by our 2000+ volunteers".

17 December 2025: Environment Agency signs 'death warrant' for river Wye say campaigners: Campaigners for the Wye are accusing the Environment Agency of doing too little to save their river. Voice of the River Wye's Louise Bodnar, said: "This is a death warrant for the Wye, it's sickening that those with the power to help the river choose not to. For the many farmers who want to improve the river this is a crushing blow. It describes how everything currently available in stewardship schemes won't be enough for the river to recover, but offers no support to do more".

16 December 2025: Another worrying "report card" from the warming Arctic: The Arctic is transforming faster and with more far-reaching consequences than scientists expected just 20 years ago, when the first Arctic Report Card assessed the state of Earth's far northern environment. The snow season is dramatically shorter today, sea ice is thinning and melting earlier, and wildfire seasons are getting worse. Increasing ocean heat is reshaping ecosystems as non-Arctic marine species move northward. Thawing permafrost is releasing iron and other minerals into rivers, which degrades drinking water. And extreme storms fueled by warming seas are putting communities at risk.

16 December 2025: Why we need to treat freshwaters as a network: It's time to stop thinking about waterbodies in isolation and start focusing on whole freshwater landscapes, says Freshwater Habitats Trust CEO Jeremy Biggs: "When we talk about freshwater conservation, it's natural to picture a healthy river, a pristine pond, or a thriving wetland. That's exactly how most people have traditionally approached protecting freshwater life – focusing on single habitats as if they exist in isolation. But wildlife doesn’t see the world that way. If we're serious about reversing the decline in freshwater biodiversity, we need to fundamentally change how we think about – and protect – our freshwater environment".

16 December 2025: First water voles spotted in River Thames in decades: Volunteers from the River Thame Conservation Trust (RTCT) have recorded vole activity on the River Thame near Chearsley, Buckinghamshire, and on Chalgrove Brook in Stadhampton, Oxfordshire.

16 December 2025: Plastic alert in the River Irwell: Bio beads, likely from a sewage works in Manchester, have prompted a pollution investigation by the Environment Agency.

16 December 2025: Past and future change in global river flows: A detailed review in the scientific journal Nature, from Lukas Gudmundsson and colleagues, summarizes the current understanding of past and projected changes in global river flow, focusing on annual volumes, seasonal dynamics and sudden changes.

15 December 2025: Biodegradable wet wipes? Not so much!: Scientists have tested, for the first time, how biodegradable wet wipes break down when flushed rather than composted discovering that most wipes remain after five weeks – a finding the research team say challenges the marketing of these products.

15 December 2025: Tenbury pupils help restore rivers and create greener school grounds: Schoolchildren across Tenbury Wells are playing a central part in a major effort to restore rivers, improve habitats and strengthen climate resilience in the Kyre Brook area. Supported by the David and Christine Lloyd-Jones Foundation, Severn Rivers Trust is delivering a long-term programme that brings together river restoration, nature-based solutions and hands-on community involvement. Local schools sit at the heart of this work, with pupils helping to create greener school grounds while learning how healthy rivers support both wildlife and people.

13 December 2025: Your new fake lawn is making flooding worse... but there is a £30 solution: The I calls out garden pavers and suggests we can all make a difference to floods and droughts by investing in a cheap and simple water butt.

13 December 2025: Salmon seen spawning after river restoration: Great news from the North, where West Cumbria Rivers Trust have new evidence of salmon migration.

12 December 2025: Paddle UK backs Sport England over proposed planning policies: Paddle UK is backing a campaign to prevent Sport England being stripped of its role in national planning policy. Campaigners say thousands of playing fields and facilities could potentially be lost to housing if the proposals go ahead. The Sport and Recreation Alliance's Protect Where We Play campaign shows that playing fields and pitches deliver at least £2bn in improved wellbeing every year and help 4 million people to be active, including one million children.

12 December 2025: Why are the Rhee and Cam so murky?: Cam Valley Forum's (CVF) Mike Foley presented a talk on river turbidity and its most probable causes in Cambridge recently; here's a write-up of his analysis and the slides that go with it.

6 December 2025: Monitoring the ecological status of the River Worth with electrofishing: ​River Worth Friends and the Aire Rivers Trust have been monitoring the ecological status of the Worth for a number of years, using electrofishing (to count the actual number and species of fish) and riverfly monitoring (to count the number of invertebrates living in the river, on which the fish feed). In this blog post, they explain how electrofishing works and how they analyse their results.

5 December 2025: River Education Flows to Tyne Schools: Tyne Rivers Trust shares the success of their recent educational outreach, bringing the Tyne catchment area to life for local students. Their recent activities have been a fantastic blend of interactive learning, artistic expression, and local history.

11 November 2025: The future of Scotland's seas depends on fair and effective marine planning: Scotland's marine environment, in all its diversity, is used by everything from fishing to energy to recreation. Offshore wind, though not exactly new, is using larger areas of sea, and is set to expand further. Due to increasing pressures, the way that Scotland's marine environment and these sectors interact needs to be managed. Deciding "what happens where" is the role of the National Marine Plan, which is the marine equivalent of Scotland's National Planning Framework. But though Scotland’s National Marine Plan has been in place since 2015, it has largely not been followed, and the health of its seas has suffered as a result.
